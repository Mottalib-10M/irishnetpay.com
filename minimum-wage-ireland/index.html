Minimum Wage Ireland 2026: Rates, Take-Home Pay and Rules

The national minimum wage in Ireland is €14.15 an hour in 2026, for employees aged twenty and over. On a thirty-nine hour week that is €28,696 a year before deductions, and €25,107 after income tax, USC and PRSI for a single employee with the standard credits, which works out at about €2,092 a month. Those net figures come from the same calculation engine that powers the calculator on this site, so the numbers here and the numbers it produces agree. The minimum wage is a legal floor, not a recommendation: paying less is an offence, and the obligation applies whatever the contract says. It is set as an hourly rate, which matters more than it sounds, because entitlement is calculated over a pay reference period rather than per shift. What follows sets out the reduced rates for younger workers, what actually counts as pay for the purpose of meeting the floor, and how the minimum compares with the living wage.

The difference between the two is €1,318 a year gross, but only €949 after deductions. Roughly a quarter of the gross gap disappears into tax, USC and PRSI, which is worth knowing before treating the two hourly rates as directly comparable measures of living standards.

At this level of income the income tax bill is small, because tax credits reduce it directly rather than reducing taxable income. USC and PRSI still apply and account for most of what is deducted. Someone on the minimum wage for a full year stays well below the standard rate cut-off point, so none of that income is taxed at the higher rate.

These figures assume a single employee with the standard credits and no pension contribution. Change any of those and the net changes: the calculator takes filing status, pension rate and additional credits, and applies the same engine used here.

The rate rose from €9.80 in 2019 to €14.15 in 2026, an increase of 44 per cent across seven years. The three largest rises fall in 2023, 2024 and 2025, reflecting a period of high consumer price inflation and a stated policy of moving the floor towards the living wage.

This is the nominal rate, not what it buys. A rise that trails inflation is a cut in real terms, and comparing this series with consumer prices is the only way to tell which years were which. That comparison needs a different dataset and is outside what this page covers.

Rates shown are those in force at the start of each year. Increases usually take effect on 1 January following the Budget, though that has not always been the case, so a rate that changed mid-year may appear here under the year it began rather than the year it was announced.

What counts as pay towards the minimum

This is where disputes usually start, because not everything an employer pays counts towards the floor.

Basic pay counts, as do shift premiums, productivity bonuses and, within limits set in regulation, board and lodging provided by the employer. Overtime premiums, tips, expenses, allowances for special duties and public holiday premium payments do not count towards the minimum, even though they are real money in the employee's pocket.

The practical consequence is that an employee whose total pay looks comfortably above the floor can still be underpaid once the non-reckonable elements are stripped out. Anyone checking a payslip should therefore compare the reckonable pay against the hours worked in the pay reference period, not the gross total against the hourly rate.

The pay reference period

Entitlement is not assessed shift by shift. The employer sets a pay reference period, which cannot exceed one month, and the test is whether average reckonable pay across that period meets the hourly minimum.

That has a consequence people find counter-intuitive: a single week paid below the rate is not automatically a breach if the average across the reference period clears it. Conversely, averaging cannot be used to justify a persistent shortfall.

An employee is entitled to ask their employer in writing for a statement of average hourly pay for a given reference period, and the employer must supply it. That statement is the starting point for any complaint, and it is worth requesting before escalating anywhere else.

Where these figures come from

The hourly rate and the reduced percentages are statutory. The gross annual equivalents are our own arithmetic, the hourly rate multiplied by thirty-nine hours and fifty-two weeks, and are labelled as such wherever they appear.

The net amounts are produced by this site's own calculation engine, applying the 2026 income tax bands, tax credits, USC rates and PRSI. Our methodology page sets out exactly which rates are used and where each comes from.

Statutory rates change, typically at the start of a calendar year following the Budget. If you are reading this well into a later year, check the current rate before relying on it: a page that quietly keeps last year's figure is worse than no page, and that is precisely the mistake this one was written to correct.

The sub-minimum rates for employees under twenty

Irish law still permits lower statutory rates for younger employees, set as percentages of the full national minimum wage: those aged under eighteen may be paid seventy per cent of it, those aged eighteen ninety per cent, and those aged nineteen ninety per cent as well under the structure introduced in 2019. From the twentieth birthday the full rate applies, and the employer is expected to move to it in the pay period in which the birthday falls rather than at the end of the year. These are ceilings on what may lawfully be deducted from the adult rate, not target rates: nothing prevents an employer from paying the full rate to a seventeen-year-old, and many collective agreements require it.

Two points about the sub-minimum rates are frequently misunderstood. They are age-based rather than experience-based, so a nineteen-year-old with three years of service is still legally payable at the reduced rate while a twenty-year-old on their first day is not. And the training rates that used to allow reduced pay during structured training were abolished in 2019, so an employer citing a training rate for an adult employee is relying on a provision that no longer exists. The Low Pay Commission has recommended the phasing out of the remaining age-based rates, and any change would come through an amending order rather than through negotiation.

Deductions an employer may and may not make

The minimum wage is a floor on gross pay, and the Payment of Wages Act governs what may come off it. Statutory deductions, income tax, USC and PRSI, are always permitted. A deduction required or authorised by the contract, such as a pension contribution the employee has agreed to, is permitted if the contract was given in writing in advance. A deduction for something provided by the employer, such as accommodation or a uniform, is permitted only within limits the Act sets, and only where it is fair and reasonable in the circumstances.

What may not come off is anything that has the effect of taking pay below the statutory minimum for time actually worked. Till shortages, breakages, customer walk-outs and the cost of required uniforms cannot be recovered from wages where doing so would breach the floor, and a deduction for an over-payment must be agreed in writing and spread so as not to breach it either. Employers must keep records of hours and pay for three years, and in a Workplace Relations Commission complaint the absence of those records is taken against the employer rather than the employee.

Who enforces the rate, and how a complaint proceeds

Enforcement sits with the Workplace Relations Commission, whose inspectors may enter a workplace, examine records and interview employees without notice. An individual complaint is made on a standard form within six months of the contravention, and an adjudication officer holds a hearing at which both sides may be represented. The remedy is payment of the arrears, and the officer may also order compensation where the breach was accompanied by penalisation. Appeals go to the Labour Court within forty-two days, and a determination may be enforced through the District Court. Nothing in the process requires the employment to have ended, and dismissal for having made a complaint is itself actionable.

Two practical notes on evidence. Keep a personal record of hours worked, since the statutory three-year record-keeping duty falls on the employer and a dispute about hours is far easier to win with a contemporaneous note than without one. And ask for the written statement of terms that the Terms of Employment Information Act requires within five days of starting: it states the hourly rate, and its absence is itself a separate contravention.

Common questions

Can an employer pay less than the national minimum wage?

Only in the narrow cases the Act allows: close relatives of the employer, people on a statutory apprenticeship, and those under twenty, who have their own sub-minimum rates. Outside those cases the rate is a floor that no contract, collective agreement or consent can lower, and an agreement to accept less has no legal effect.

Do tips count towards the minimum wage?

No. Since the Payment of Wages Amendment Act 2022, tips and gratuities cannot be used to make up the minimum wage, and they must be distributed fairly and transparently. Service charges presented to customers as going to staff must be paid to staff. An employer counting tips towards the hourly floor is in breach on both counts.

What happens if I have been underpaid?

A complaint can be made to the Workplace Relations Commission within six months of the underpayment, extendable to twelve where there is reasonable cause. The adjudication can award arrears, and the Act makes penalisation for having made a complaint a separate offence. Records must be kept by the employer for three years, and their absence counts against them.

Does the minimum wage apply to part-time work?

Yes, at exactly the same hourly rate. The rate is hourly, so it applies irrespective of the number of hours worked, and there is no threshold below which it ceases to apply. What changes with part-time work is only the weekly and annual total, along with the point at which PRSI and USC thresholds are crossed.

Does it apply during a trial or probation period?

Yes. There is no probationary exemption in Irish law, and an unpaid trial shift is unlawful where work of value is performed. Training required by the employer counts as working time and must be paid. The only sub-minimum rates that exist are age-based and apply to employees under twenty, regardless of probation.

Is the living wage a legal obligation?

Not yet. The living wage is a target figure, calculated on the cost of a minimum essential standard of living, towards which the statutory minimum is being raised in steps. Until the transition is complete, only the national minimum wage is legally enforceable, and the living wage figure serves as a benchmark rather than a right.
