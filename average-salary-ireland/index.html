Average Salary in Ireland 2026: What the CSO Data Shows

Average weekly earnings across all sectors in Ireland stood at €1,046.88 in 2026Q2, according to the Central Statistics Office. Multiplied across fifty-two weeks, that is €54,438 a year before tax. Average hourly earnings were €31.96, over an average paid week of 32.8 hours. Those three numbers come from the same official release and describe the same workforce, which is why they are worth reading together rather than separately: the hourly figure looks high precisely because the average paid week is well under forty hours, and any comparison with a full-time salary elsewhere has to account for that. The headline figure is also an average across every employee in the economy, from part-time hospitality staff to full-time software engineers. It is not what a typical worker earns, it is not a median, and it is not a salary you should expect to be offered. What it is good for is comparison over time and between sectors, and that is how this page uses it.

The headline numbers, and what each one measures

The annual equivalent is the weekly figure multiplied by fifty-two. It is arithmetic, not a measurement: the CSO publishes earnings weekly and hourly, not annually. Treating the result as a salary assumes fifty-two paid weeks, which holds for most salaried employees in Ireland since annual leave is paid, but not for seasonal or irregular work.

The gap between the hourly and weekly figures is the part most readers miss. At €31.96 an hour, a standard thirty-nine hour week would produce roughly €1,246.44. The published weekly average is lower because the average paid week is 32.8 hours, pulled down by part-time employment, which is concentrated in particular sectors rather than spread evenly.

The spread between sectors is far wider than any change over time, which is why a single national average tells you so little about your own position.

Information and communication sits at €1,730.61 a week, accommodation and food service at €506.78. That is a ratio of more than three to one between the highest and lowest sectors published. Someone working in hospitality and someone working in software are both included in the €1,046.88 national average, and neither would recognise it as describing their pay.

Part of the gap is hours rather than rates. Accommodation and food service averages twenty-eight paid hours a week against the national 32.8, so the weekly figure understates the hourly rate in that sector. Comparing sectors on weekly earnings alone therefore overstates the difference in pay per hour, though a substantial difference remains after that adjustment.

Average weekly earnings rose from €771.39 in 2019Q2 to €1,046.88 in 2026Q2, an increase of 35.7 per cent across seven years. That is the nominal change: it says nothing about what those euros buy. Comparing it with consumer price inflation over the same period is the only way to tell whether pay has kept pace, and that comparison is outside what this dataset supports.

The two largest annual rises in the series fall in 2020 and 2024. The 2020 figure deserves particular caution: employment composition changed sharply that year, and when lower-paid roles leave the measured workforce the average rises without anyone receiving a pay increase. An average is sensitive to who is counted, not only to what they are paid.

Why the average is not the typical salary

Three things separate the published average from what a given person earns, and all three push in the same direction.

It is a mean, not a median. A small number of very high earners lifts a mean well above the midpoint of the distribution. More people earn below the average than above it. The CSO release used here publishes means; anyone quoting it as what most people earn has misread it.

It mixes full-time and part-time. The 32.8 hour average paid week is the clearest evidence of this. A figure that blends a thirty-nine hour contract with a fifteen hour one describes neither.

It is national, not local. Sector composition differs across the country, and the sectors that pay most are not evenly distributed. A national average carries that composition with it.

From gross average to what actually arrives

A gross figure is not take-home pay. On €54,438 a year, an employee in Ireland pays income tax at the standard and higher rates depending on where the standard rate cut-off point falls, plus USC and PRSI, and receives tax credits that reduce the bill directly rather than reducing taxable income.

The difference between the gross headline and the net amount is large enough that comparing job offers on gross alone is unreliable, particularly across the cut-off point where each additional euro is taxed at the higher rate. Our take-home pay calculator applies the current bands, credits, USC rates and PRSI to a gross figure and shows what remains, and our methodology page sets out exactly which rates it uses and where they come from.

Where these figures come from

Every number on this page comes from the Central Statistics Office release Average Earnings, Hours Worked, Employment and Labour Costs, dataset EHQ03, for 2026Q2, retrieved from the CSO public data API on 26 September 2026. Sector figures are for all employees within each NACE Rev. 2 sector.

Annual equivalents are our own arithmetic, weekly earnings multiplied by fifty-two, and are labelled as such throughout. The CSO does not publish an annual average salary figure, and any site presenting one as official has calculated it the same way without saying so.

The CSO revises quarterly earnings data as later returns arrive, so figures for recent quarters can change. This page states the quarter and the retrieval date precisely so that a reader can check it against the current release rather than trusting it indefinitely.

What a national average cannot tell you

Three structural features of the Irish labour market make the national average less informative here than in most comparable economies, and all three are visible in the sector table above. The first is the weight of foreign-owned multinational employment, concentrated in information and communication, pharmaceuticals and financial services. Those sectors pay well above the national figure and employ a small share of the workforce, which pulls the average up without moving the middle of the distribution. The second is the size of the accommodation and food sector, at the other end of the table, where a large number of employees work part-time hours at close to the statutory minimum. The third is geography: earnings in Dublin run materially above the national average, and the gap is larger in the high-paying sectors than in the low-paying ones, so a national figure understates the capital and overstates everywhere else.

The practical consequence is that comparing your own salary to the national average answers almost nothing. The useful comparison has three coordinates rather than one: the sector, the region and whether the hours are full-time. The CSO publishes each of these breakdowns separately, and the sector table on this page is the one that moves the number most. A second comparison worth making is against the same sector two years ago rather than against other sectors today, because that is the figure a pay review is implicitly measured against.

How Ireland compares, and why the comparison is difficult

Ireland ranks high among European countries on average gross earnings, and the ranking is real but flatters the position in two ways. Eurostat figures are gross and before tax, and Ireland combines a relatively low effective tax rate on average earnings with an unusually high cost of housing, so gross earnings translate into a smaller advantage in disposable terms than the ranking suggests. And national accounts measures such as GDP per head are distorted in Ireland by the accounting treatment of multinational activity, which is why the Central Statistics Office publishes a modified gross national income series specifically to correct for it.

For an individual comparison, the measure that survives these problems is net earnings after housing cost, and it is the one that international rankings rarely publish. Anyone weighing a move should therefore compute the figure themselves: take the gross offer, run it through a net pay calculation for the country in question, and subtract a realistic rent or mortgage for the specific city rather than a national average. On that basis the gap between Irish cities and several continental ones narrows considerably, and in some cases reverses.

One last caution applies to any single-figure comparison. Average earnings are a flow measured over a quarter, while a salary offer is an annual commitment, and the two differ whenever bonuses are seasonal. In Ireland the first quarter carries most bonus payments in financial services and the fourth carries most in retail, which is why the same sector can appear several percentage points apart between two quarters of the same year. Comparing the same quarter across years, as the series above does, removes that effect and is the only way to read a trend.

Common questions

Is the average salary in Ireland a good salary?

It is the average, which means a majority of employees earn less. Whether it is good depends entirely on sector, hours and location, and the sector table above shows a spread of more than three to one. A better question is what the average is in the sector you work in, which is why that breakdown is on this page.

Why is the average hourly rate so high compared with the weekly figure?

Because the average paid week is under thirty-three hours, not thirty-nine. Part-time employment pulls the weekly average down without affecting the hourly rate. If you work full-time hours, the hourly figure is closer to your reality than the weekly one.

Does the figure include bonuses and overtime?

Yes. The CSO series used here covers total earnings, which includes irregular earnings such as bonuses and overtime. The CSO also publishes a series excluding irregular earnings, which runs lower and is the better measure for comparing base pay between sectors.

How often is this page updated?

The CSO publishes this series quarterly. This page states the quarter it reflects and the date the figures were retrieved, and both appear in the section above so you can tell at a glance whether a newer release exists. Revisions to recent quarters are normal as later returns arrive, so a figure can move slightly after publication.

Is the average the same for men and women?

No. The CSO publishes a separate gender pay gap measure, and the gap persists across most sectors once hours are controlled for. The earnings series used on this page is an all-employee average and does not break down by sex, so it should not be read as a typical salary for either group.

How does the average compare with the median?

The median is lower, because a minority of very high earners pulls the average up. Ireland's earnings distribution is more skewed than most of western Europe, largely because of the concentration of multinational employment in a few sectors, so the gap between average and median is wider here than the figures alone suggest.
